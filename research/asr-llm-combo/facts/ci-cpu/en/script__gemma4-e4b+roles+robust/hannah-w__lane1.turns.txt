therapist: Hello Hannah, come in and take a seat. What would you like to talk about today?
client: I'm not really sure where to start. I think I'm just anxious all the time, and it's got worse since September.
therapist: What happened in September?
client: I was made deputy head at my school. I've taught Year 4 for eleven years and I loved it, and then the head asked me to step up when our deputy left.
therapist: That sounds like recognition of your work.
client: That's what everyone says. But I keep thinking they picked the wrong person, and that sooner or later someone will notice I have no idea what I'm doing.
therapist: What does it look like when that thought arrives?
client: Mostly Sunday evenings. My chest goes tight and I start rehearsing Monday's staff meeting in my head.
therapist: What do you rehearse?
client: Every possible question someone might ask, and what I'd say. Then what they'd think of me if I got it wrong.
therapist: And what do you imagine they would think?
client: That I'm out of my depth. That they gave the job to a nice Year 4 teacher who can't actually lead.
therapist: Has anyone said anything that suggests that?
client: No. That's the strange thing. Nobody has. It's all in my head.
therapist: It is still very real to you.
client: Very. Last week I rewrote the same email to the governors four times.
therapist: Four times. What was the email about?
client: A change to the behaviour policy. It was two paragraphs. I was afraid Mr Hargreaves, the chair of governors, would think I was stupid.
therapist: How does he treat you?
client: He's been nothing but kind. He told me in September he was delighted. That makes it worse somehow, because I feel I'm lying to him.
therapist: Lying to him about what?
client: About being capable. As if I'm pretending.
therapist: Pretending to be what?
client: Somebody who knows what she's doing. Most days I feel like a child wearing her mother's coat.
therapist: That's a vivid image. Whose coat is it?
client: My mum's, I suppose. She was a headteacher for twenty years.
therapist: Tell me about that.
client: She was brilliant. Everyone in the village still stops me to say what a wonderful head she was. She retired six years ago and they've named a library after her.
therapist: And you're now in a similar job.
client: A deputy's job. But yes. I can't help comparing. Everything I do, I think, would she have done it better?
therapist: Does she say that?
client: Never. She's very supportive on the surface. She says I've always worried too much and I should stop being silly.
therapist: How does it land when she says that?
client: It makes me feel I'm making a fuss. So I don't tell her how bad it is.
therapist: What does she not know?
client: That I'm awake at four every morning. I fall asleep fine, then I'm awake at four going through the to-do list.
therapist: What's on the list?
client: Everything. Timetable changes, the Ofsted prep, a parent complaint, a child I'm worried about. It goes round and round.
therapist: How long have you been waking like that?
client: Since about the second week of term. I drink a lot of coffee to get through the day, which I know doesn't help.
therapist: How many cups?
client: Five or six. And then I can't sit still in the afternoons.
therapist: What do you notice in your body during those afternoons?
client: My heart goes fast. My hands are a bit shaky. Last Thursday I had to leave a meeting to splash my face with water.
therapist: Did anyone notice?
client: I don't think so. I said I was fetching a document.
therapist: That sounds like a lot of effort to hide it.
client: It is. I'm exhausted by the end of every day just from pretending.
therapist: Who knows how you are feeling?
client: My partner Ben knows a bit. He says I should just enjoy it.
therapist: How do you feel when he says that?
client: Guilty, and a bit alone. He means well. He's an engineer, he thinks if something's a problem you fix it.
therapist: And you can't just fix this.
client: No. I wish it were that simple.
therapist: Do you have friends at school you can talk to?
client: Not any more, not really. I used to eat lunch in the Year 4 corridor with the others. Now I'm their line manager and it's awkward.
therapist: So you've lost some company along with the promotion.
client: Yes. I hadn't thought of it like that. I miss them. I miss my class too.
therapist: What do you miss most?
client: The children, honestly. The daily bit where a child gets something for the first time. Now my days are spreadsheets.
therapist: Did you want the promotion?
client: I thought I did. I said yes in the room because I couldn't say no to her. The head. Then I went home and cried.
therapist: What were the tears about?
client: Fear, I think. And that I'd never get to go back.
therapist: Would you want to go back?
client: I don't know. That sounds terrible, doesn't it? After everyone's been so supportive.
therapist: It sounds like an honest question.
client: Nobody has asked me that. They just say well done.
therapist: When was the last time you felt properly relaxed?
client: The summer holidays. We went walking in the Lakes, and I didn't think about school once for ten days.
therapist: What was different there?
client: Nobody expected anything of me. I was just walking. Ben carried the sandwiches.
therapist: And then?
client: The week before term I was a wreck again. I couldn't eat on the Sunday.
therapist: Is there anything you have already tried that helped, even a little?
client: I started writing down what went well each evening. Small things. A child laughing, a good email.
therapist: How did that go?
client: It helped for about two weeks and then I stopped, because I felt silly.
therapist: Silly in what way?
client: As if I were bragging to myself in a notebook.
therapist: Noticing what went well is not the same as bragging.
client: Probably not. It just felt that way.
therapist: That sounds worth coming back to. Have you thought about whether there is something particular you'd like from our work?
client: I'd like to do the job without feeling like a fraud.
therapist: What would that look like on a Tuesday?
client: Walking into a meeting and not rehearsing it first. Sending an email once.
therapist: And on a Sunday?
client: Being able to sit on the sofa in the evening without my stomach in knots.
therapist: Those are clear goals. How do you feel now, saying them out loud?
client: Lighter. A bit tearful, actually.
therapist: That's all right. Take a moment.
client: Sorry. I didn't expect that.
therapist: There's nothing to apologise for. What's coming up?
client: That it's the first time I've told anyone how bad it is. Not even Ben knows about the four a.m.
therapist: What would it be like to tell him?
client: Scary. He'd probably be hurt that I didn't say sooner.
therapist: Would that be a reason to keep it to yourself?
client: I suppose not. I might try this week.
therapist: What would help you do that?
client: If I choose a quiet moment. Not on a Sunday evening, then I'm too wound up.
therapist: Saturday morning, perhaps?
client: Yes, over breakfast. He's always in a good mood then.
therapist: And one more small thing. Would you try the evening notebook again, just for a week, even one line?
client: I could. I'll put it by the kettle so I don't forget.
therapist: We're nearly out of time. Is there anything you want to add?
client: Only that I nearly cancelled this morning. I'm glad I didn't.
therapist: I am glad too. Same time next week?
client: Yes, please. Thank you.
