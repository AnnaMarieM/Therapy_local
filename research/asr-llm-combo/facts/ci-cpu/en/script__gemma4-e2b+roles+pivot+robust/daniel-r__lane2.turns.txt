therapist: What does exhausted look like for you?
client: Like I'm running on fumes. I work as a project manager at a logistics company, and I've been completely burned out since October.
therapist: Since October. Did something happen then?
client: Not one thing. We lost a big client, and half the team got moved onto the rescue plan, so I ended up doing three people's jobs. I just never came back up after that.
therapist: What does a normal working day look like now?
client: I'm at my desk by seven and I'm still answering messages at ten at night. Even when I'm not working I'm thinking about work.
therapist: That's a very long day. How is your sleep?
client: Terrible. Most nights I sleep about four hours. I wake up at three in the morning and can't fall back asleep.
therapist: What happens when you wake at three?
client: My head just starts. Shipping schedules, the client, whether I've forgotten something. Then I look at the clock and do the maths on how little sleep I'm going to get, which makes it worse.
therapist: And then?
client: Then I lie there until about five and get up and make tea. I've started dreading the nights, if I'm honest.
therapist: That sounds draining. Is there anything else going on in your life besides work?
client: Yes. My father. He was diagnosed with Parkinson's disease in the autumn.
therapist: That's a lot to carry alongside everything else. How is he?
client: He's still himself, mostly. His hands shake, and he's slower, and he gets frustrated. He's seventy-three and he's always been the strong one.
therapist: And how are you involved in his care?
client: I'm the one who drives him to the hospital in Leeds. The neurology appointments are every few weeks, and it's an hour each way.
therapist: Is there anybody who shares that with you?
client: Not really. My younger sister Hannah lives in Toronto, so all the care for our father falls on me.
therapist: How do you feel about that?
client: Guilty for even saying it. She calls him every Sunday, and she's not the one who's neglecting him. It's just that I'm the one who's here.
therapist: Can you say more about the guilt?
client: I feel it's selfish to be tired when he's the one who's ill. So I don't say anything to anyone.
therapist: Not to Hannah either?
client: She asks how I am and I say fine. What would be the point? She can't do anything from Canada.
therapist: What would you want her to know, if you said it?
client: That I'm not fine. That some days I sit in the car park at the hospital for ten minutes before I go in, because I can't face it.
therapist: That's an important thing to notice. What happens in those ten minutes?
client: Nothing, really. I just breathe. I look at the other people going in. Everybody looks tired there.
therapist: You mentioned your GP. What did she say?
client: She did blood tests, and they were all normal. She said it's stress and exhaustion, and asked if I'd thought about talking to someone. I said I hadn't got time.
therapist: And yet you're here.
client: My girlfriend booked it, to be honest. Claire. She said she was done watching me disappear.
therapist: How is it with Claire?
client: She's very patient. She makes me tea and doesn't push me to talk. But I can see it's wearing on her too.
therapist: In what way?
client: She stopped asking if I want to go away for the weekend, because I always say no. That's the thing I feel worst about, actually.
therapist: What stops you saying yes?
client: The emails. If I'm away I'll come back to two hundred of them. And Dad might need something.
therapist: So even rest has a price.
client: Yes, exactly. I can't remember the last time I was properly off.
therapist: What did you do for yourself before October?
client: I ran. Ten kilometres every Saturday morning. I'd done it for six years.
therapist: And now?
client: I stopped about two months ago. My legs felt like lead, and then I just never went back.
therapist: How do you feel about that?
client: Like I've lost a part of myself. It was the one time in the week nobody could reach me.
therapist: What would it mean to get some of that back?
client: A lot, probably. I just can't picture where the time would come from.
therapist: Let's leave that for a moment. What is your relationship with your boss like?
client: Peterson. He's all right as a person, but he sends emails at eleven at night and expects an answer before breakfast.
therapist: What do you do when one arrives?
client: I answer it. Every time. Even if I'm in bed. Claire hates it.
therapist: What are you afraid would happen if you didn't answer?
client: That he'd think I wasn't committed. There's a restructuring in the spring and I'm afraid they'll let me go.
therapist: And if they did?
client: I don't know. I think part of me would be relieved, and then I'd feel horrible about being relieved. I have a mortgage.

client: I suppose so. I've never said that out loud.

client: Flat, mostly. And irritable. I snapped at a colleague last week over nothing and went and sat in the toilets for ten minutes.

client: My heart was going and my hands were shaking. I thought for a second it was a heart attack, but it passed.

client: A couple of times. Once on the train to Manchester. I just had to get off and stand on the platform.

client: That I'm losing it. That I'm going to end up like, I don't know. Not coping.

client: Uneasy. It works for an hour and then I wake at three anyway. I know it's not helping.
