therapist: Good day, Mr. Barto, please continue. How are you doing today?
client: Good day. I am quite nervous, if I am to be honest. I have never been to a psychologist.
therapist: That is absolutely fine. What brings you here?
client: My wife. She says it can't go on like this. And I actually don't either.
therapist: What can't go on?
client: Thirty times a night I check if I locked the door and turned off the stove, and it takes me an hour to leave the apartment.
therapist: An hour. What does it look like?
client: I lock the door, I go to the elevator, I come back and pull the handle. Then I check the stove, every knob separately. Then the door again.
therapist: And when you do that, what happens?
client: I feel relieved for a while. And then an image comes into my head that I didn't do it properly, and I have to do it again.
therapist: What kind of image is it?
client: The apartment on fire. I see it clearly, like a film.
therapist: How often does it happen to you?
client: Every day. It's the worst in the evening when we go out or go to sleep. In the morning too.
therapist: How long has this been going on?
client: It started two years ago when the neighbors' house caught fire and we had to evacuate.
therapist: Tell me more about it.
client: It was at night. The alarm woke me up. There was smoke in the hallway and people were screaming. We ran out just in our pajamas.
therapist: How did you feel then?
client: That this was the end. No one was hurt, but the neighbors' house burned down completely. I stood outside in the winter and watched the windows burn.
therapist: And since then?
client: Since then I have the feeling that I can cause it. That if I don't control something, it will happen and I will be to blame.
therapist: That is a great responsibility.
client: I know it doesn't make sense. I know the logic. But I can't turn it off.
therapist: What do you do when you don't want to do it?
client: I try to let it go. And then anxiety grows in me, until I feel like I'm going crazy. I'd rather do it.
therapist: How does the anxiety manifest?
client: Heart, sweat. My hands shake and I feel sick in my stomach.
therapist: Who knows about it?
client: Barbora. My wife. At first she took it as a habit, now she sees that it's more.
therapist: How does she react to it?
client: She is patient, but I see that it is exhausting her. Yesterday we arrived at the cinema forty minutes later.
therapist: What happened?
client: She waited outside for me to return and check the stove for the third time. She didn't say anything, she just looked at me.
therapist: How did you feel?
client: Shame. I felt like a child who can't leave the house.
therapist: What do you do for work?
client: I work as an electrician in Olomouc. I do wiring in apartments and sometimes in houses.
therapist: How is that related to checking?
client: A lot. I'm afraid that because of my head I will make a mistake once. That I will connect a wire incorrectly and something will burn.
therapist: Has anything like that happened to you?
client: No, never. But after every job I go back and check. Sometimes I go back the next day, just to look.
therapist: And your colleagues?
client: They think I'm a pedant. The boss tells me I'm the most careful in the company, but he doesn't see how much time it costs.
therapist: How much time does it cost?
client: In the last few months, it adds an hour of work to me every day. I have already handed over a few jobs to a colleague because I didn't have time.
therapist: What is that doing to you?
client: I'm afraid I'll lose my job. Especially now.
therapist: Why right now?
client: Barbora is four months pregnant.
therapist: Congratulations. How did you take it?
client: I was happy. Really. We waited for it for three years. And right after that another thought came.
therapist: What kind?
client: That I will harm her and the baby.
therapist: How will you harm them?
client: Not that I want to. It's terrible, I would never do it. But the thought comes, maybe when I'm cutting bread or standing by the stairs. Then I'm afraid it will happen on its own.
therapist: So it's a thought that scares you.
client: Yes. It's the worst. I've never told anyone about it, not even Barbora.
therapist: Thank you for telling me. That is important.
client: Do you think I am dangerous?
therapist: Such intrusive thoughts are very common in people with this type of problem, especially when a big change is approaching. They are not proof that you would do something. Their strength is precisely in how they scare you.
client: That helps me. Still, it takes time before I believe it.
therapist: I understand. What do you do when that thought appears?
client: I leave the room. Or I go back and check something to make it stop.
therapist: So checking helps you calm this too?
client: Actually, yes. It never occurred to me that they were related.
therapist: How do you sleep?
client: I sleep terribly. I wake up twice at night and check the stove, even if there is nothing on it.
therapist: Does anything help you?
client: Going for walks with Barbora helps me. Or when I fix something at home, it keeps me busy.
therapist: How often do you do that?
client: Walks twice a week, repairs when there is something.
therapist: What would it mean to you if it got better?
client: To leave the apartment in five minutes. And to look forward to a child without those thoughts.
therapist: Those are clear goals. Do you know there is treatment that helps with that?
client: I heard about therapy where a person is gradually exposed to what they are afraid of. I was afraid of that.
therapist: Fear is understandable. It would be in small steps and you would decide the pace.
client: That is reassuring. I thought someone would force me to leave the door open and go.
therapist: No, it wouldn't. We could start by you checking the door once, not three times.
client: That sounds small. But even that is big for me.
therapist: Big steps start with small ones. You said something important about Barbora. What would you like to tell her?
client: That I love her. And that I need to get treated so I don't lose her. Right now I'm afraid she wouldn't want to be with me.
therapist: What do you think she would say?
client: She would probably hug me. That's how she is.
therapist: Maybe it's worth telling her at least part of it. What if you told her that you were here and what you learned?
client: I could handle that. Not those thoughts yet.
therapist: That's okay. You set the pace.
client: Thank you. This gave me more than I expected.
therapist: Until next time, try one thing: when you leave the apartment, check the door once and write down how cramped you felt.
client: Once. I'll try it. At least once, and I'll write down a number from one to ten.
therapist: Exactly. We'll finish here for today.
client: Thank you, see you next week.
