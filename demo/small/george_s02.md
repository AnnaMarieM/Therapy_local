# George — Session 2 — 2026-02-04

Therapist: Hello, George. Come in. It's horrible out there, isn't it?

George: It's awful. I parked on the wrong side of the road and then had to walk back through the puddle by the bins. My good shoes are ruined, which is ridiculous, I know.

Therapist: It isn't ridiculous. Sit wherever's comfortable. How has your week been?

George: [pause] Busy. Same as usual, I suppose. Busy in the way where you don't really stop, you just keep going until it's dark and then you look up and it's half six and everyone's gone except Dennis's light is still on.

Therapist: So the same pattern we talked about last time.

George: Yes. Worse, maybe. I don't know. It's hard to tell from inside it. Everything feels a bit more like it's on fire than it was a few weeks ago, but that might just be because it's February and we're all tired.

Therapist: How's Milo?

George: Milo is Milo. He got into the kitchen bin on Monday and pulled out a yoghurt pot and a bit of kitchen roll. Then on Tuesday he chewed the corner off Laura's library book. Not the whole book, just the corner, but she was quite upset about it, and I was upset that she was upset, so we ended up having this whole stupid conversation about dogs' teeth and whether he's got a problem.

Therapist: Did he have a problem?

George: [laughs] No. He's six. He's got the teeth of a six-year-old beagle who eats things he shouldn't. The vet said so in November. But we both went and looked at the book with the little bite marks in it and sort of agreed that it wasn't really about the book.

Therapist: What do you think it was about?

George: [pause] I don't know. I think she'd had a long day with a class who wouldn't sit still, and I'd had a long day, and neither of us wanted to be the one who said so. So we talked about the dog instead. It's easier to argue about a dog.

Therapist: That's an interesting observation.

George: Is it? It's just what happened.

Therapist: It sounds like you noticed the thing underneath, which is quite a skill. Let's come back to Laura in a bit, because I'd like to hear more about that. Can I ask first how the sleep has been?

George: Still four o'clock. I've tried everything. The app, the lavender thing, not having the phone in the room. It doesn't matter. I wake up at four, my brain starts going, and then I'm lying there doing the same spreadsheet in my head for an hour and a half. Then the alarm goes and I feel like I've been hit by a bus.

Therapist: The spreadsheet in your head. What's on it?

George: The engagement schedule for the Hartley account, mostly. Whether I've put the right partner sign-off on the second tab. Whether Dennis is going to ring me at nine and tell me the whole thing has to be redone. [pause] And sometimes it's not a spreadsheet at all. Sometimes it's just a voice telling me I've missed something obvious and everybody has noticed.

Therapist: Whose voice is it?

George: [pause] I don't know that it's anybody's, really. It's just the voice. It's probably a bit of Dennis and a bit of, I don't know. It's the voice that tells you you're not good enough and you'd better fix it before anyone finds out.

Therapist: So it's a critical voice. Something that says you're not good enough.

George: Yes. That's a fair description.

Therapist: I'm noticing that when you describe it, you take it quite seriously. You don't say it's silly, even though you said earlier you'd been thinking about whether it's your problem or the work's.

George: [sighs] I suppose I do take it seriously. I've been taking it seriously for about thirty years, so it's a bit late to stop now.

Therapist: Thirty years. That's a long time to be listening to a voice.

George: [laughs] Yes. It's a long time.

Therapist: Let's go there a bit, if that's all right. Last time we talked about the work, and you mentioned your father almost in passing. You said that when you were talking about the criticism at the office, you could hear him. Do you remember that?

George: I remember I said something about it. I'm not sure I meant it. Dad's been retired for ages, he doesn't have much to do with the day to day now. But yes, I think I did say it, and then I spent the whole week thinking about it, which I wasn't very pleased about.

Therapist: What did you think about?

George: [pause] School mostly. Which is annoying, because I'm forty-one and I should be able to think about my father without going back to Year Nine.

Therapist: Year Nine. Tell me about Year Nine.

George: It was a physics test. Circuits, Ohm's law, all that. Mr Pritchard, he was a lovely man, very patient, always had a Jaffa Cake in his top drawer. I'd done quite a lot of revision, which I did because I was scared of the test, to be honest. I got nine out of ten. And I was pleased. I remember walking home across the park with the paper folded in my blazer pocket, feeling quite light, like I'd got away with something.

Therapist: How did you feel when you walked in?

George: I didn't walk in straight away. I hung about on the front step for a bit because I wanted to be sure I'd got the lighting right in my head. Then I went in and Mum was in the kitchen, and I said, I got nine out of ten in physics, and she said, oh, love, that's brilliant. Then Dad came in from the garage, he'd been sort of fiddling with the lawnmower, and she said, George got nine out of ten, and he took the paper off me and read it through.

Therapist: Read it through.

George: Properly. He always read everything properly. He read the question with my working, and he got his pen out, and he went through it. Then he put the pen down and he said, "Where did the other point go?"

Therapist: [pause] Where did the other point go.

George: Yes. [pause] And that was it. He didn't say anything about the nine. He didn't say well done. He didn't even say it was a good mark, or that Mr Pritchard had probably marked it hard. He just wanted to know where the other point had gone, so I stood there and tried to think what the question was, and I couldn't really remember which one I'd got wrong, and that made it worse, because then I had to go and look.

Therapist: So you went and looked.

George: I went upstairs and I sat on my bed with the textbook for about three hours. Dinner was on the table and I didn't come down, and Mum sent Claire up with a plate, and Claire was very kind about it, which I hated. In the end I found it. It was a question about resistance in parallel, and I'd put the wrong unit on the final answer. Ohms instead of, I don't know, something else. Just a stupid unit. So I'd lost a mark for writing the wrong word at the end.

Therapist: And what did you do with that?

George: I wrote it on a sticky note and put it on the inside of my pencil case. It's probably still there, if my mum hasn't thrown it away. [laughs] I think I was quite proud of that, actually, in a sad way. I remember thinking, right, I'll never do that again.

Therapist: You remember that clearly.

George: I remember it very clearly. I could draw you the kitchen. The lino was orange. There was a calendar from the Bristol Harbour Festival on the wall, we'd never been to it, I don't know why we had it.

Therapist: The calendar from a festival you'd never been to. [pause] I'm wondering what it was like, living in that house, with that calendar and that lino, and a father who read your work so carefully.

George: [pause] It was normal, I think. That's the odd thing. I don't think anyone would've called it a bad childhood. We had holidays. We went to Cornwall every August in the same caravan. Dad taught me to change the oil in the car. He used to make me explain things to him, which was annoying at the time, but I suppose it was sort of nice, too, because he was listening.

Therapist: He was listening.

George: Yes. He was very good at listening, if you were telling him something he could check. He didn't like anything he couldn't check.

Therapist: Can you say more about that? Something he could check.
