# Marta — Session 2 — 2026-02-24

Therapist: Good morning, Marta. Come in, sit wherever feels right. You look a little tired, if I can say that.

Marta: Good morning. Yes, you can say it, I know. I put the concealer on this morning, the expensive one Aisha told me about, and it is still obvious. [laughs] I did not sleep much. Three hours, maybe three and a half. After four o'clock I stopped looking at the clock, because looking makes it worse.

Therapist: Three and a half hours is very little to get through a day on.

Marta: It is not really a day, it is more like a fog. I feel like a radio between two stations, you know, the sound goes in and out. Pepa was on my chest at five, which was nice, and then at half past five she decided breakfast was now, and that was the end of my sleeping.

Therapist: Pepa is your cat.

Marta: Yes, Pepa. Grey, very bossy, from the shelter in Levenshulme. I got her about two years ago, after Tomas and I finished, and I think she is the only one in my life who has never disappointed me. [laughs] That is a strange thing to say in therapy, I know.

Therapist: I don't think it's strange. She sounds like a comfort to you. The flat must feel quiet at the moment.

Marta: Quiet, yes. In Bratislava there was always someone. The neighbours upstairs with their television, the trams outside, Mama in the kitchen with the radio on, always the radio, the same station, the one with the old songs. Here in Chorlton there is only the fridge and the radiator, and the radiator knocks like somebody at the door. I have lived here three years and I still think it is someone at the door.

Therapist: So the knocking starts, and for a second it feels as if someone is there.

Marta: Yes. And then I remember that nobody is coming. Which is stupid, because I am a grown woman with a job and a rent and a cat. Nobody is supposed to come.

Therapist: I don't think it's stupid. It's what happens when a place has been full of someone, and then it isn't.

[pause]

Marta: Mm.

Therapist: Last time we spoke a little about work. How has the week been, apart from the sleep?

Marta: Busy. Busy-busy. Greenfield Health has the pilot for the medication reminder app running in three clinics, one in Salford, one in Stockport, one in Wythenshawe, and I am the one doing the interviews. So every day is eight or nine patients, and then in the evening I write it all up. The notes are everywhere. On my kitchen table, in my bag, in the bathroom, one under the sofa, I think. Sam keeps saying I am doing great. Sam always says I am doing great. It is kind, but I don't trust it. Sam would say I am doing great if I was on fire.

Therapist: You don't quite trust Sam's reassurance.

Marta: Not completely. Last Thursday I sent the product team a summary with a table where the columns were in the wrong order. Date, then patient ID, then the site, then the date again, it was all mixed up. Nobody said anything in the meeting. But afterwards Aisha looked at me in the kitchen, by the coffee machine, and she said, "Marta, you never make mistakes like this." And I felt terrible, because she was right. And also I was angry that she noticed. Which is not fair to her. She is only my friend.

Therapist: You were angry at Aisha for noticing.

Marta: Not at her. At me, I think. I am supposed to be fine. I am supposed to be the organised one, the one with the plan and the spreadsheet with the colours. And I am not fine, and the table shows it. Is that strange?

Therapist: It sounds understandable to me. When the work slips, it can feel like evidence of something else, something you'd rather keep outside the room.

Marta: Yes. Exactly that. Sometimes I think I am scared that if I stop being efficient for one single day, everything comes out at once. Like when the bag of onions in the shop splits at the bottom and they roll everywhere. [laughs] Sorry, that is a silly picture.

Therapist: It's a clear picture. You can keep it.

Marta: Okay. I keep it.

[pause]

Therapist: Could we go back to the sleep for a moment? You said three hours. What happens when you wake up at three?

Marta: Always three. Like an alarm clock somebody else set and I cannot find the off button. I look at the phone and it says 3:04, or 3:11, or 3:02. I know the numbers by heart now. And then the phone call starts again in my head. From the beginning, always from the beginning. I hear the traffic in the background, I hear the car door when I got in, I hear myself saying the words.

Therapist: Let's stay with that for a moment, if you're willing. The words you're saying to yourself.

Marta: [pause] Can I ask you something first?

Therapist: Of course.

Marta: Do we have to do the phone call today? Because I was thinking I would talk about the sleep and the work, and then maybe that is enough for today. I don't feel ready. Last time I told you about the flight, and the hospital, and the nurse with the bad perfume, and I walked out of here with a headache for the whole day. Aisha had to make me tea.

Therapist: Yes, you did tell me that, and I'm glad you did. You're allowed to decide how close we go today. We don't need to go to the bottom of it in the first ten minutes. If you'd like, we can talk about the sleep and the work first, and then I'll come back gently to the phone call at a point you choose. Does that feel workable?

Marta: Yes. Thank you. I know it is your job to ask, and I know it is important. I just... my body goes like this. [she presses her hands together in her lap]

Therapist: I can see that. Your hands have gone very still.

Marta: [laughs quietly] Yes. Mama used to say I got that from Babka. Babka holds her hands exactly like this when she is in church and the priest is too long.

Therapist: Babka Ruzena.

Marta: Yes. She is eighty-six now. She is still in Ružinov, in the same flat for fifty years, she won't move, not even for the lift, which they put in last year and she refuses to use. She phones me on Sundays, always on Sundays at four o'clock, and her first question is always the same. "Are you eating?" Every Sunday. Not "how are you", not "how is the work", "are you eating." Last week I said, "Babka, I am eating," and she said, "What did you eat?" And I said, "Soup." And she said, "What soup?" And I said, "The one from the packet, Babka." And she made a noise like a dog who has been promised a walk and then didn't get one.

Therapist: [laughs softly] She's keeping an eye on you from a distance.

Marta: From the other side of Europe. She has a radar, she knows everything. And she is right, this is the problem. I am not eating well. I skip lunch, I forget, and then in the evening I eat bread with butter standing up in the kitchen, not a meal, just something so that the feeling stops for a little bit.

Therapist: The feeling of being hungry?

Marta: Hungry, or the empty feeling. Maybe it is the same thing. I stopped cooking, you know this already. Since the summer I have not really cooked. Before, I used to cook on Sunday, the soup and the bread and the potatoes with the caraway, all the things Mama made. Now I buy the packet soup, like Babka says, which is a terrible crime against Slovak cooking. But I don't want the kitchen to smell like that. Like her.

Therapist: The smell of cooking brings her into the room.

Marta: Yes. Too much. Sometimes just the onions in the pan, and I have to go and sit on the floor of the bathroom with my back against the bath. So I stopped. It is simpler.

Therapist: It sounds as if simpler has become the way you get through the days.

Marta: Simpler is the way I get through the days. It is not the way I would choose if I had a choice. [pause] Sorry, that sounded angry.

Therapist: It didn't sound angry to me. It sounded honest.

Marta: Okay. Honest. That is better, I think.

[pause]
