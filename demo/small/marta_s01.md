Marta: Yes. That sounds clear. Sorry, is it forty minutes or fifty? The email said fifty.

Therapist: Fifty. I'll keep an eye on the time.

Marta: Good. I like people who keep an eye on the time. My manager Sam is like that, he has a timer for everything. Not for me, I mean in meetings. He is very kind, but the timer is always on the table.

Therapist: You mentioned Sam in your form. Tell me a little about work, if you like. But you don't have to start there, we can start wherever feels right.

Marta: Work is fine, work is the easy part. I work at Greenfield Health. It is a startup, health tech, we make an app for people with long-term conditions, diabetes, some heart things. Medication reminders, a little bit of tracking, the usual. My job is to talk to users and find out what is broken. So I sit with people and I ask them to show me how they use the app, and then I watch them struggle. It is actually very interesting. People are very creative in how they get things wrong.

Therapist: That sounds like it takes real skill, watching someone struggle without rescuing them.

Marta: Yes. That is exactly the skill. You have to not help. My mother would have helped everybody, she could never watch anyone struggle with a jar lid. [pause] Sorry. That came out. I did not plan to talk about my mother first.

Therapist: That's all right. We can come back to her. Did you want to start with her?

Marta: I think yes. I think that is why I am here, even if the form says sleeping and so on. The sleep is real. But it is because of her. [pause] Sorry, I need a moment.

Therapist: Take all the time you need.

Marta: [pause] Okay. It is strange to say it in a room. I have said it to my friends, to Aisha, to my brother on the phone, but not in a room with a person whose job is to listen.

Therapist: It can feel different. Can I ask, what is your mother's name?

Marta: Elena. Elena Novakova, before she married my father she was Elena Hrubá, but nobody calls her that. Everybody calls her Elena or Mama, or, for the neighbours, pani Novaková. She was fifty-eight. She died on the nineteenth of July last year. Twenty twenty-five. So it is seven months ago, almost exactly seven months, because today is the tenth of February. I counted on the calendar this morning. I do this sometimes. I count.

Therapist: You counted this morning, before you came.

Marta: Yes. I count the days in the mornings. It is not every day now, maybe every few days. In August it was every day. I had a little notebook where I wrote the number. Then I stopped the notebook because it felt silly. But I still count in my head.

Therapist: Would you like to tell me a bit about her? Who she was.

Marta: She was a seamstress. Not in a fashion house, she had a little workshop at the back of the flat, in Petržalka. Actually no, we lived in Ružinov, in the block by the tram loop, but the workshop was in the bigger room, with the big table and the machine, the old Singer, the black one with the gold writing. People came to her for wedding dresses, for christenings, for trousers that needed taking in. She was very good. She could look at you and know your measurements without a tape. It was almost frightening.

Therapist: She sounds very skilled.

Marta: She was. And she was warm, very warm, she was the warmest person I know. She sang while she cooked. Always. Old songs, folk songs from her village, and some of the Karel Gott songs that my father hated. She would sing very loudly and off key and then laugh at herself. Michal, my brother, used to hide in his room when she sang, and then he would come out and sing with her, but pretend he didn't.

Therapist: It sounds like the house was full of music.

Marta: Yes. Full of noise. It was never quiet in our flat. My father worked at the railway depot, shifts, so he was often asleep in the afternoon, and then there was my mother with the radio and the sewing machine and the pots, and me and Michal fighting. I think it was a happy house. Mostly. [pause] I am not sure I should say mostly. It was happy.

Therapist: You said "mostly," and then you corrected yourself.

Marta: Yes. I always correct myself. My English teacher said it is the Slovak in me, we are very careful about what we say about our families. Or maybe it is the researcher in me, I always want to say the accurate thing. [laughs] Do you want the accurate thing or the nice thing?

Therapist: I'm interested in what's true for you. Both can be true.

Marta: Okay. The true thing is we did not talk very much in my family about feelings. My father is very quiet. My mother talked a lot, but about the shop, the neighbours, the price of thread, not about how she felt. You knew she loved you because she made you a jacket. That was the language.

Therapist: A jacket as a way of saying something.

Marta: Yes. A jacket, a skirt, a costume for the school play. She made my graduation dress. Blue, with the little collar. I wore it for the ceremony in Bratislava and then I wore it again in Manchester, once, to a party, and everybody asked where I got it and I said my mother, and they said "she is a genius," and I said yes, she is. [pause] Sorry.
